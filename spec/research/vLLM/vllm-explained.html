A TUTORIAL FOR CURIOUS KIDS (AND GROWN-UPS)

vLLM, explained with a Word Kitchen

There is a famous program called vLLM whose whole job is to let one AI brain answer a whole restaurant of hungry question-askers at the same time. Ten short chapters. One story. Lots of pictures. No scary math.

The whole tutorial in one sentence vLLM is a set of clever kitchen tricks — smart shelf space, a never-idle chef, a shared speech card, a magic pencil, and a universal plug — that let one expensive AI brain serve a crowd without dropping plates.
CHAPTER 01

What is vLLM?

In one sentencevLLM is a free program whose whole job is to let one AI brain answer very many people's questions at the same time, quickly and without wasting memory.

Three words you need

1. An AI brain (LLM). Imagine a friend named Ada who has read almost everything ever written. Ask her a question and she answers. Strange part: she writes one word at a time. Word... word... word...

2. A GPU. Answering takes millions of tiny multiplications. A normal chip does a few at a time; a GPU does thousands at once — like swapping one rolling pin for a hundred rolling pins together.

3. Serving. One Ada, one friend: easy. Two hundred people at once? You need a kitchen manager. That manager is vLLM.

Grown-up word

An AI brain = an LLM (Large Language Model) — Llama, Qwen, Mistral, DeepSeek… The fast math chip = a GPU. The kitchen-manager job = serving (or "inference serving"). vLLM is open source and is a PyTorch Foundation project.

Check the evidence

Up to 24× more answers per hour than the naive method and 3.5× vs the previous best kitchen (HuggingFace TGI) — see RES-31 Exhibit 02 [E1], independently re-tested by an arXiv study [E18].

Check yourself
  1. Does vLLM include an AI brain?
  2. Why does Ada write one word at a time instead of a whole paragraph?
  3. Name the two wasteful things vLLM fixed.
Answers: 1) No — you bring the model; vLLM manages it. 2) Each next word depends on all the words before it, so the answer grows one token at a time. 3) Wasted shelf space (Ch 03) and the idle chef waiting for full batches (Ch 04).

CHAPTER 02

Why AI computers get stuck

In one sentenceThe chef must keep notes about everything she has read; the notes eat counter space; the old way of saving space for notes wasted most of it.

The chef's scratchpad

To write the next word, Ada must remember everything before it. Re-reading the whole conversation for every single word would be thousands of times too slow — so she keeps a scratchpad: after reading each word once she writes super-short notes about it. Next word? One quick glance at the notes.

The trouble: the notes grow with every word. A short question, a thin notepad. "Remember these hundred pages" — a notepad the size of a mattress. The counter (GPU memory) is big but not infinite.

The old fix that made everything worse

Old kitchen managers said: "Every order gets a locker for its notes. Some orders are huge… so make every locker mattress-sized, just in case." Sounds safe. Look what it does:

Grown-up word

The scratchpad = the KV cache. The wasted air has two names: internal fragmentation (locker too big for its notes) and reservation waste (space pre-allocated for a maximum-length answer that never gets that long). Old systems pre-allocated fixed maximum lengths per request.

Check the evidence

The waste numbers and the fixed-locker model are described in the vLLM project's founding paper/blog [E1] — RES-31, Section 03.

Check yourself
  1. Why doesn't the chef just re-read your question every time?
  2. What eats the GPU's counter space?
  3. Why were the old lockers mattress-sized?
Answers: 1) It would be thousands of times slower — the scratchpad turns a full re-read into one small glance. 2) The KV cache — notes that grow with every word of every conversation. 3) Managers reserved the longest-possible conversation for every order, just in case.

CHAPTER 03

PagedAttention: the parking-lot trick

In one sentenceInstead of one giant fixed locker per order, vLLM cuts note space into tiny numbered slots and hands them out as needed — like small marked parking spaces instead of spaces sized for trucks.

How much more fits?

The vLLM team measured it: ~24× more answers per hour than the naive locker system, and ~3.5× more than the previous pro manager (TGI). Years later, university researchers re-tested it under heavy crowds — same story. Not a lucky demo; it is how the trick works.

Grown-up word

The trick is PagedAttention. Small numbered slots = KV blocks. An order's notes = a list of block numbers, exactly like an operating system manages memory in pages — that is the joke in the name: paging + attention.

Check the evidence

Numbers from the vLLM announcement [E1] and the Nov 2025 arXiv re-test [E18] — RES-31 Exhibit 02. Honest footnote: this fixes space, not the idle chef — that is Chapter 04.

Check yourself
  1. What do the small numbered slots stand for?
  2. Can two orders share slots? When?
  3. PagedAttention fixes space. What does it NOT fix?
Answers: 1) KV blocks — small chunks of the chef's notes. 2) Yes — if their prompts begin with identical text, the shared part points at the same blocks. 3) Idle-chef waiting — that needs Chapter 04.

CHAPTER 04

Continuous batching: the chef never waits

In one sentenceOld kitchens made the chef wait for a whole busload to finish together; vLLM slides the next order in the instant one plate leaves — the chef is always cooking.

The bus problem. Old rule: wait for 30 orders, cook them as one group, wait until the whole group is done. Disaster: one kid asked "2+2?" (one word), another asked for a full essay (a thousand words). The chef — the most expensive thing in the building — sits idle waiting for the essayist.

Grown-up word

Continuous batching, a.k.a. iteration-level scheduling. The crowd numbers come from the MDPI 2026 study "Benchmarking Ollama and vLLM for Concurrent LLM Serving" [E8]; Ollama's default of 1 parallel order is in its FAQ [E9]; its fast single-user answer (~45 ms) is from Ginger Labs [E10]. All in RES-31 Exhibit 03.

Check yourself
  1. What is wrong with "wait for a full bus" batching?
  2. When does vLLM start the next order?
  3. You alone at home, one question at a time — does this trick help you?
Answers: 1) Everyone waits for the slowest order; the expensive chef idles. 2) The instant any single order finishes. 3) Mostly no — crowd tricks need a crowd; a simple home kitchen is fine for one person (Chapter 09).

CHAPTER 05

Prefix caching: write the boring part once

In one sentenceIf every order starts with the same long introduction, vLLM keeps the chef's notes for that introduction on the shelf and reuses them — the reading is paid for once, not hundreds of times.

Every restaurant makes you sit through the same speech: "Welcome, here are the hours, the allergy chart, how ordering works…" Imagine that speech is six pages and every customer must hear all of it before their real question. For Ada the speech is real work: she reads the pages and writes scratch notes. One hundred customers = one hundred identical speeches. Ugh.

vLLM's trick: she reads the six pages once, keeps those notes on the shelf, and every new customer skips to the good part — pointing at the same notes (thanks, Chapter 03!). And since 2025, keeping that shelf costs vLLM almost zero effort.

Grown-up word

The shared speech = the prompt prefix; the trick = automatic prefix caching, made "near-zero-overhead" in vLLM's rebuilt v1 engine (Jan 2025) [E2]. The 30K/6K numbers are SpecForge's task model [A5] — RES-31, Sections 06–07.

Check yourself
  1. What gets reused — the chef's answer, or her notes?
  2. Why does Chapter 03 make this trick possible?
  3. When is prefix caching useless?
Answers: 1) Her notes (the KV cache of the shared opening) — answers are always unique. 2) Identical openings can point at the same small slots instead of being copied. 3) When every request is different from the very first word.

CHAPTER 06

Structured outputs: the magic pencil

In one sentenceHand vLLM a mold — a form, a pattern, or a whole file format — and the robot becomes physically unable to write an answer that breaks it.

Ada improvises. Ask for "yes or no" and she might write a paragraph. Ask for a data file and she might forget a bracket. Charming… until another computer has to read the answer. vLLM's fix: while Ada types word by word, a teacher stands behind her with a hand on the keyboard and quietly blocks any key that would break the form. Wrong keys never press.

Grown-up word

This is structured output (guided / grammar-constrained decoding): at each step, tokens that would break the target format are masked out before sampling. The default engine is xgrammar; the mightiest mold takes an EBNF/GBNF grammar [E3]. SpecForge's grammar-mold idea is RES-31 Section 07, building on RES-29.

Check the evidence

Feature and mold types: official vLLM structured-outputs docs [E3]. Honest caveat: masks can shift word choices inside the legal ones, so RES-31 gates the grammar gift on a measured trial (KPI STR-1: 500 generations, 100% must parse) before "recommended".

Check yourself
  1. How does vLLM stop the robot from breaking the form?
  2. Which mold is mightiest?
  3. With a grammar mold, is the robot guaranteed to be right — or just well-formed?
Answers: 1) Illegal next tokens are masked out before picking — wrong keys cannot press. 2) grammar — it can enforce a whole file format. 3) Just well-formed; meaning needs specforge check.

CHAPTER 07

The universal plug — and treasure maps

In one sentencevLLM speaks the same request language as almost every AI tool on Earth, so you can swap kitchens by changing one line — and it can also turn sentences into map points that show which meanings are neighbors.
Grown-up word

The letter format = the OpenAI-compatible REST API (/v1/chat/completions, /v1/embeddings) [E3][E16]. Map points = embeddings; "who is nearby" = cosine similarity; vLLM serves embedding models and pair-scoring on the same plug [E15]. SpecForge's EmbeddingProvider socket is defined in spec/extensions/embeddings/ports.spec.

Check yourself
  1. Why did everyone adopt the same letter format?
  2. Your program talks to vLLM today; you want SGLang tomorrow. How much rewriting?
  3. Two sentences land near each other on the meaning-map. What does that mean?
Answers: 1) A shared format lets any tool work with any kitchen — the common one became the standard. 2) Change the address — one line. 3) Their meanings are similar — that is semantic search.

CHAPTER 08

Speed and money

In one sentenceKitchens are judged by four numbers — plates per hour, wait for the first bite, gap between bites, dropped plates — and one money question: rent the food truck, or pay per burger?

And the mapmaker's bill (Chapter 07)? Mapping SpecForge's entire recipe book — about 2,000 recipes — costs roughly half a million words, which at a burger stand is one cent. Which is why embeddings alone never justify buying a truck.

Grown-up word

Throughput, TTFT, TPOT, success rate — the four KPIs of the RES-31 dossier, Section 04, each with a target and a measurement recipe [E4][E5][E8]. Burger prices [E13], truck prices [E11], the task model and break-evens [A1–A4], the one-cent map [A6] — all computed in RES-31 Exhibit 05.

Check yourself
  1. Which number dominates how fast a website feels?
  2. Roughly when does the truck beat GPT-4.1 burgers?
  3. What reason beats any price for renting the truck?
Answers: 1) TTFT — the wait for word #1. 2) Around 3,100–5,000 work orders a month (burst schedule). 3) Privacy: self-hosted = zero source code leaves the building.

CHAPTER 09

The great kitchen race

In one sentencevLLM is not the champion of everything — it is the most balanced all-rounder: near the front in almost every race, and the steadiest pick once you need a crowd, molds, and the universal plug.

So who wins WHICH race?

CHAPTER 10

vLLM and SpecForge: two doors and a gift

In one sentenceSpecForge is a recipe-checker, not a kitchen — so vLLM can only enter through two side doors, and the best move is a gift: publishing SpecForge's grammar as a mold so any vLLM kitchen writes perfect recipes automatically.

SpecForge turns messy human intentions into .spec recipe files — a strict, checkable format — and hands AI agents a clean, validated map of the project so they stop guessing. The checker itself is deliberately boring: parse, check, export. No AI inside. House Rule 7: "make it an extension, never a built-in." House Rule 8: "value in 60 seconds, zero setup." A GPU kitchen breaks both — so it can never live in the toolbox. But two side doors are wide open:

Why the gift beats the doors

It costs zero GPUs. It helps every agent in the world, not just teams with big machines. It is vendor-neutral (any kitchen that speaks molds — vLLM, SGLang, llama.cpp — can use it). And it follows the house rules: the mold is an artifact the extension ships, not engine code inside the compiler.

One honest caveat, so you learn to think like an engineer: the mold guarantees shape, never sense — and mold-writing can slightly shift which legal words the robot picks. So the grown-up dossier gates the gift on a measured trial (500 generations, 100% must parse clean) before calling it "recommended" instead of "experimental". Guessing is not measuring.

Grown-up word

Door 1 = an adapter behind the EmbeddingProvider port (spec/extensions/embeddings/ports.spec). Door 2 = a self-hosting guide for specforge infer batches. The gift = the generation-side grammar from crates/kernel/tree-sitter/grammar.js — the mirror of RES-29's parsing grammars. Verdict: conditional adopt — RES-31 Sections 05–09.

Check yourself
  1. Why can vLLM never live inside SpecForge's compiler?
  2. What are the two doors, and who is each for?
  3. What does the mold guarantee, and what does it NOT?
Answers: 1) House rules 7 & 8: extensions over built-ins; value in 60 seconds with zero setup — a GPU kitchen breaks both. 2) Door 1: meaning-search for teams with private recipes. Door 2: self-hosted kitchens for big infer batches — privacy, then price. 3) Shape (it always parses), not sense (specforge check judges meaning).
